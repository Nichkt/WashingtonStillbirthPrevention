Washington should teach every pregnant parent to track their baby's movements

About 1 in 175 U.S. pregnancies ends in stillbirth. Around a quarter of those babies die because the placenta stops delivering enough oxygen, and a baby in that situation often moves less for a few days first. Iowa and Norway started telling parents to keep track of movement and call when it drops. We want Washington to do the same thing statewide.

What happened in Iowa and Norway

32%

Iowa's stillbirth rate fell 32% in the first ten years of Count the Kicks. The national rate barely moved over the same period. Iowa had 231 stillbirths in 2008, the year the program started.

About half

Fourteen hospitals in Norway gave every pregnant woman the same handout on fetal movement and gave staff guidelines for what to do when someone reported less of it. Among women who came in with reduced movement, stillbirths dropped from 4.2% to 2.4%. The number of those visits didn't go up.

What that could mean for Washington

1,200 families over the next 10 years saved from tragedy

Washington loses about 360 to 440 babies to stillbirth each year. If we matched Iowa's 32% drop, that would mean roughly 115 to 140 more babies coming home every year, or about 1,200 families over a decade.

Based on Washington Department of Health vital statistics: 363 fetal deaths in 2024 and 437 in an earlier year. Fetal deaths are losses at 20 weeks or later. This is an estimate, and Washington's own results would depend on how the program is run.

What we want the state to do

Count the Kicks was started in 2008 by five Iowa mothers who had lost babies. It gives clinics free printed materials and gives parents a free app for tracking movement in the third trimester. Washington providers can already order the materials, but the state doesn't run a program to get them into every clinic. We're asking for two things.

1. The Department of Health partners now

A federal law passed in 2024 lets states spend their Title V maternal and child health grant on stillbirth prevention. The Department of Health could join Count the Kicks and get materials into prenatal clinics, birth centers, WIC offices and tribal clinics. No new state law is needed for this. Kansas budgeted about $9,000 a year when it did it.

2. The Legislature passes a law in 2027

A law keeps the program going when budgets get tight. We'd like a 2027 bill that funds it, gives providers guidance on what to do when a parent reports less movement, pays for translated materials, and has DOH report stillbirth rates by county and by race and ethnicity every year.

About a quarter of stillbirths come from placental disease

23.6%

The National Institutes of Health ran the largest U.S. study on what causes stillbirth. Placental disease caused 23.6% of the stillbirths in it. Among babies who died before labor started, it was 26%, more than any other cause. If you add other pregnancy complications, the total comes to about half.

A failing placenta sends the baby less oxygen and food, and the baby slows down to save energy. Researchers who examined placentas from pregnancies where the mother noticed less movement found they were smaller and had more damage than normal. In many stillbirths, the baby moved less for three or four days beforehand.

Those few days matter. If a parent notices and calls, the hospital can monitor the baby's heart rate and do an ultrasound the same day. If the placenta is failing, doctors can deliver the baby.

Causes of U.S. stillbirth

Share of 512 stillbirths with a probable or possible cause, NIH Stillbirth Collaborative Research Network, JAMA 2011. Some had more than one cause. Other studies have put placental causes as low as 11% and as high as 65%, depending on how they sort cases.

A UK study of 301 stillbirths found that in nearly half, the mother had noticed less movement or none. Those cases were about 2.8 times as likely to involve a failing placenta.

Washington looked at this in 2023

Senate Bill 5501 from the 2023-24 session would have set up a state Count the Kicks program through the Health Care Authority. Nobody testified against it at its hearing. It went to the Senate Ways & Means Committee without any money attached and never came out. Two of its four sponsors are no longer in the Senate, so we need new legislators to pick it up.

  1. Jan 2023: SB 5501 (2023-24) introduced by Senators Keiser, Randall, Saldaña and C. Wilson.
  2. Feb 7, 2023: Hearing in Senate Health & Long Term Care. No one testified against it.
  3. 2023 to 2024: Bill sits in Ways & Means until it expires.
  4. July 2024: Federal Maternal and Child Health Stillbirth Prevention Act becomes law. States can now use Title V money for this.

Who let this bill die

SB 5501 (2023-24) was sent to the Senate Ways & Means Committee and never got a hearing. Sen. Christine Rolfes chaired the committee in 2023, and Sen. June Robinson has chaired it since 2024. Committee chairs decide which bills are heard. Washington has lost hundreds of babies to stillbirth every year since.

Sen. Robinson still chairs Ways & Means. Ask her to give a Count the Kicks bill a hearing in 2027. You can find her contact information in the Legislature's Senate member directory.

FAQ

Is this medical advice?

No. Ask your provider how and when to track movement in your pregnancy, and call them if your baby is moving less.

Won't this make parents anxious?

Doctors raised that concern in both places. In Norway, visits for reduced movement didn't increase after the handout went out, and surveys didn't find more worry among mothers.

Don't doctors already tell people this?

Some do. It depends on the clinic, and the advice isn't always the same. A state program would make sure everyone hears it.

Who is behind this site?

Washington parents.

Are some families at higher risk?

Yes. Nationally, Black families have stillbirths at more than twice the rate of white families, and the rate for Native families is also higher. We want the materials translated and handed out at WIC offices and tribal clinics as well as OB offices.

How to count your baby's kicks

Start counting every day at the beginning of the third trimester, around 28 weeks, or earlier if your provider tells you to. It takes most people a few minutes a day.

Pick a time when your baby is usually active

Try to count at the same time every day. Many people choose the evening, or after a meal.

Get comfortable

Sit with your feet up or lie on your side, somewhere quiet where you can pay attention to the baby.

Time how long it takes to feel 10 movements

Kicks, rolls, jabs and swishes all count. Start a timer when you begin and stop it at the tenth movement.

Write it down and compare

After a few days you'll see how long your baby usually takes. That pattern is what you're watching. Every baby is different, so compare your baby only to your baby.

When to call your provider

Call your provider or go to labor and delivery right away if it takes a lot longer than usual to feel 10 movements, if your baby is moving less, or if you feel a change in how your baby moves. Don't wait until the next day or your next appointment.

Don't try to get the baby moving with cold water or sugary drinks first. Research has moved away from that idea, and it can cost time. Babies should keep moving all the way through the end of pregnancy and into labor. The kind of movement may change as they run out of room, but it shouldn't stop or slow way down.
