Washington should teach every pregnant parent to track their baby's movements

About 1 in 175 U.S. births is a stillbirth. Around a quarter of those babies die because the placenta stops delivering enough oxygen, and a baby in that situation often moves less in the days or weeks before. Iowa and Norway started telling parents to keep track of movement and call when it drops. We want Washington to do the same thing statewide.

What happened in Iowa and Norway

32%

Iowa's stillbirth rate fell 32% in the first ten years of Count the Kicks. The national rate barely moved over the same period, and a 2023 study found no similar drop in neighboring Illinois, Minnesota or Missouri. Iowa had 231 stillbirths in 2008, the year the program started, and 148 in 2018.

One-third

Fourteen hospitals in Norway gave every pregnant woman the same handout on fetal movement and gave staff guidelines for what to do when someone reported less of it. Their stillbirth rate fell by about a third, from 3.0 to 2.0 per 1,000 births. Among women who came in with reduced movement, stillbirths dropped 43%, from 4.2% to 2.4%. The number of those visits didn't go up.

What that could mean for Washington

1,200 families over the next 10 years saved from tragedy

Washington loses about 360 to 440 babies to stillbirth each year. If we matched Iowa's 32% drop, that would mean roughly 115 to 140 more babies coming home every year, or about 1,200 families over a decade.

Based on Washington Department of Health fetal death data: from 2015 to 2024, Washington recorded between 363 (2024) and 441 (2016) fetal deaths a year. Fetal deaths are losses at 20 weeks or later. This is an estimate, and Washington's own results would depend on how the program is run.

What we want the state to do

Count the Kicks was started in 2008 by five Iowa mothers who had lost babies. It gives clinics free printed materials and gives parents a free app for tracking movement in the third trimester. Washington providers can already order free materials, but the state doesn't run a program to get them into every clinic. We're asking for two things.

1. The Department of Health partners now

The Department of Health gets a federal Title V Maternal and Child Health Block Grant every year. A federal law passed in 2024 says states can spend it on stillbirth prevention, and it names tracking and awareness of fetal movements. The Department could partner with Count the Kicks and get materials into prenatal clinics, birth centers, WIC offices and tribal clinics. No new state law is needed. Kansas's health department announced this kind of partnership in October 2024.

The 2024 law didn't come with new money, and much of the grant already pays for other work, including local health departments. Stillbirth prevention will only make the list if people ask for it. Here's how to ask.

2. The Legislature passes a law in 2027

A law keeps the program going when budgets get tight. We'd like a 2027 bill that funds it, gives providers guidance on what to do when a parent reports less movement, pays for translated materials, and has DOH report stillbirth rates by county and by race and ethnicity every year.

Ask the Department of Health to use Title V for stillbirth prevention

The Department of Health takes public comments on its Title V Maternal and Child Health Block Grant by email. Its priorities for 2025 to 2030 include prenatal care, postpartum visits, breastfeeding and home visiting, but they don't mention stillbirth. A short email asking the Department to add stillbirth prevention and partner with Count the Kicks takes five minutes.

About a quarter of stillbirths come from placental disease

23.6%

The National Institutes of Health ran the largest U.S. study on what causes stillbirth. Placental disease was a cause in 23.6% of the stillbirths it fully evaluated. Among babies who died before labor started, it was 26%, more than any other cause.

A failing placenta sends the baby less oxygen and food, and the baby slows down to save energy. Researchers who examined placentas from pregnancies where the mother noticed less movement found they were smaller and had more damage than normal. In many stillbirths, the baby moved less in the days or weeks beforehand.

That window matters. If a parent notices and calls, the hospital can monitor the baby's heart rate and do an ultrasound the same day. If the placenta is failing, doctors can deliver the baby.

Causes of U.S. stillbirth

  • Obstetric complications29.3%
  • Placental disease23.6%
  • Genetic or structural13.7%
  • Infection12.9%
  • Umbilical cord10.4%
  • High blood pressure9.2%
  • Maternal medical7.8%

Percent of all 512 fully evaluated stillbirths with each probable or possible cause, NIH Stillbirth Collaborative Research Network, JAMA 2011. A cause was found for 76% of them, and some had more than one. Other studies have put placental causes as low as 11% and as high as 65%, depending on how they sort cases.

A UK study of 301 stillbirths found that in nearly half, the mother had noticed less movement or none before the stillbirth was found. When she had noticed less movement, the odds of a failing placenta were about 2.8 times higher.

Washington looked at this in 2023

Senate Bill 5501 from the 2023-24 session would have set up a state Count the Kicks program through the Health Care Authority. Nobody testified against it at its hearing. The Health & Long Term Care Committee passed it, but the Ways & Means Committee never gave it a hearing. Its fiscal note estimated $683,000 for the first two years, mostly for the Health Care Authority, and $100,000 every two years after that.

Of its four sponsors, only Sen. Claire Wilson will still be in the Senate in 2027, so the bill needs new champions.

  1. Jan 2023: SB 5501 introduced by Senators Keiser, Randall, Saldaña and C. Wilson.
  2. Feb 7, 2023: Hearing in Senate Health & Long Term Care. Four people testified for it and no one testified against it.
  3. Feb 16, 2023: The committee passes it and sends it to Ways & Means.
  4. 2023 to 2024: No hearing in Ways & Means. The bill dies when the 2024 session ends on March 7.
  5. July 2024: The federal Maternal and Child Health Stillbirth Prevention Act becomes law. States can now use Title V money for this.

What we can do now

The next chance to pass a law is the 2027 session. The work that decides whether a bill moves happens before it starts.

  1. Now: Ask your senator and representatives to sponsor a Count the Kicks bill. Ask the Department of Health to use its federal Title V money for a statewide program, which doesn't need a vote.
  2. December 7, 2026: Bill prefiling opens. Legislators, including newly elected members, can file bills before the session starts. A bill filed early has more time to get a hearing and a cost estimate.
  3. January 11, 2027: The session starts. This is a 105-day session, and lawmakers write the next two-year budget, so it's the best time to get money for the program.
  4. February 19 and 26, 2027: Last days for bills to pass policy committees (February 19) and budget committees like Ways & Means (February 26). Parents and providers can testify in Olympia or remotely.
  5. March 10, 2027: Last day for bills to pass the chamber where they started.
  6. April 14, 2027: Last day to pass the other chamber.
  7. April 25, 2027: Last day of the session. Bills that don't pass stay alive for the 2028 session, but the two-year budget is written in 2027.

FAQ

Is this medical advice?

No. Ask your provider how and when to track movement in your pregnancy, and call them if your baby is moving less.

Won't this make parents anxious?

Doctors raised that concern. In the Norway project, visits for reduced movement didn't go up after the handout went out. In a separate Norwegian trial of about 1,000 women, those who counted movements reported slightly less worry than those who didn't, and about as many in each group were ever concerned about their baby moving less.

Don't doctors already tell people this?

Some do. It depends on the clinic, and the advice isn't always the same. A state program would make sure everyone hears it.

Who is behind this site?

Washington parents.

Are some families at higher risk?

Yes. In 2024, U.S. stillbirth rates were about 10 per 1,000 births for Black families and for Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander families, and 7.8 for American Indian and Alaska Native families, compared with 4.6 for white families. We want the materials translated and handed out at WIC offices and tribal clinics as well as OB offices.

How to count your baby's kicks

Start counting every day at the beginning of the third trimester, around 28 weeks, or earlier if your provider tells you to. It takes most people a few minutes a day.

  1. Pick a time when your baby is usually active

    Try to count at the same time every day. Many people choose the evening, or after a meal.

  2. Get comfortable

    Sit with your feet up or lie on your side, somewhere quiet where you can pay attention to the baby.

  3. Time how long it takes to feel 10 movements

    Kicks, rolls, jabs and swishes all count. Start a timer when you begin and stop it at the tenth movement.

  4. Write it down and compare

    After a few days you'll see how long your baby usually takes. That pattern is what you're watching. Every baby is different, so compare your baby only to your baby.

When to call your provider

Call your provider or go to labor and delivery right away if it takes a lot longer than usual to feel 10 movements, if your baby is moving less, or if you feel a change in how your baby moves. Don't wait until the next day or your next appointment.

Don't try to get the baby moving with cold water or sugary drinks first. Research has moved away from that idea, and it can cost time (Count the Kicks FAQ). Babies should keep moving all the way through the end of pregnancy and into labor. The kind of movement may change as they run out of room, but it shouldn't stop or slow way down.
